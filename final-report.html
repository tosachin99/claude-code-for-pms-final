Rook Dispatch, Release 4.2: what it did, to whom, and the way back

Release 4.2 shipped on 12 August 2026 and landed badly. This report pulls together what six sessions of work found across the Rook wiki, the Rook database and the routing code, and what to do next.

1The answer in brief

  • What 4.2 changed. Two things at once: an offer now stays on a hero’s phone for 60 seconds instead of 90, and the ranking leans harder on speed (0.45 → 0.60) and less on “said yes lately” (0.40 → 0.25). OBSERVED (release notes; config.py)
  • How big. Missed offers rose from 2.3% to 18.0% and the share taken fell from 76.6% to 64.0%. The change began on release day and shows in every time of day, every position in the order and all 15 areas. OBSERVED (pings table)
  • Who it hit hardest. Four heroes (Farlight, The Undertow, Meteor Mite, Vesper) went from 306 offers in 44 days to 56 in 26. None has taken an offer since 19 August. OBSERVED
  • Why, most likely. The shorter wait turns slow answers into misses. The code scores a miss exactly like a “no” (−0.12, against +0.08 for a yes), so a hero must say yes at least 60% of the time to hold steady, and scores never ease back. The four fell to 18–29%. ESTIMATED (the database holds no scores or answer times)
  • “It is just August.” Not supported. Acceptance sat at 72–81% for six weeks, then dropped on 12 August. There is no earlier-year data to compare. OBSERVED
  • Recommendation. Give quiet heroes a visible comeback path, stop counting a miss like a refusal, and confirm the real scores with Wen Li before building. A draft brief and a clickable prototype are in the repo.

3Why it happened

How the routing code works, in plain terms, from 00-rook/code/dispatch-routing/.

  1. List who is free in the area (availability.py).
  2. Score each by speed, skills and “said yes lately” (routing.py, weights in config.py).
  3. Ask the top hero and wait 60 seconds (offer.py, config.py).
  4. A yes adds 0.08. A no or no answer takes off 0.12. The score stays between 0 and 1, and never drifts back by itself (history.py).
  • The break-even point. Holding steady needs a yes at least 60% of the time (0.12 ÷ 0.20). Before 4.2, all 16 heroes were above that (69–85%). Afterwards exactly four fell below it (18–29%). ESTIMATED arithmetic from the code and counts from the pings table
  • Misses, not refusals, did the damage. Across the 16 heroes, how often they said yes after 4.2 tracks their change in offers at 0.94, and their missed share at −0.91. How often they refused before tracks it at only −0.27. This is a small sample of 16 heroes. OBSERVED
  • Being ranked low means not being asked. About 74% of callouts end at the first offer (982 of 1,319), so a low rank rarely gets work. OBSERVED
  • Hidden by the average. The headline rate recovered to 75.2% in early September partly because the four quiet heroes are now almost absent from it. OBSERVED
  • An unexpected side effect. The share of offers going to a hero in their own area fell from 82.9% to 60.6%, and 22% of taken callouts now go to someone outside the area (0 of 831 before). The cause is not established. OBSERVED effect, ESTIMATED cause
Marcus Oyelaran’s open question (14 August): was the change meant to apply to heroes who turn jobs down? The code answers half of it: one formula, no special case for decliners, and a comment in history.py says a miss and a refusal are the same on purpose. Whether anyone intended that to meet a 60-second wait is not recorded. The sharper question for Wen Li: “Was a missed ping meant to cost the same as a ‘no’ once the wait dropped to 60 seconds?”

5The way back

The Director of Product asked for something a handler would notice and a quiet hero would feel, “not a setting.” The brief and prototype answer that.

A comeback path (draft, for the Director to react to)

  • For the hero: after a quiet stretch (length still to decide), the next suitable callout goes to them first, with a longer wait.
  • For the handler: the hero’s card says so in plain words, so the handler stops guessing.
  • Underneath: a missed offer stops costing the same as a refusal, and scores ease back toward the middle over time. This answers a 2019 note in the code that assumed silence means someone stopped taking work.

Success measures (proposals): the four back to at least 8 offers a week within three weeks; a yes-rate of 60% or more; no hero with zero offers for 7 days; new quiet-phone tickets from about 8 a week to none within four weeks.

Files: brief.md and prototype.html (download it and open it in a browser; try the Kip tab).

Orders, in priority order (suggested owners)

  1. Get the real scores and answer times, and confirm the live code matches this copy (Wen Li).
  2. Stop counting a missed offer like a “no”, and let scores ease back (Wen Li, Marcus Oyelaran).
  3. Test a longer wait on a small group (Marcus Oyelaran).
  4. Build the comeback path and the handler card (Wen Li, Sofia Marino).
  5. Reply to Linda Pruitt, Desmond Okafor, Aunt Dot and Kip now, since they should not wait for the build (Nadia Hoffmann).
  6. Write down how routing decides, which the previous PM left undone.

Not ready to build yet: the brief still has seven open gaps (section 8), and its owner line is unconfirmed.
